How to make your Google Forms more accessible

A practical guide for people who build forms but don’t write code. I picked the topic, tested every recommendation with real assistive technology, and wrote and produced the article for the Pope Tech blog.

The brief

Pope Tech publishes accessibility guidance as part of its content plan, and people regularly search for how to make the tools they already use more accessible. Google Forms is one of the most common of those tools, especially in education.

The goal: give readers changes they can make in their next form, in the Google Forms interface they already know, without needing to read WCAG first.

What testing found

  • 01

    The date picker was the biggest barrier. Google Forms uses the browser’s built-in date input, so it behaves differently depending on the browser, operating system, and assistive technology. With Voice Control in Safari, results were inconsistent.

  • 02

    Google Forms has no alt text field. The caption was the only place to put a text alternative for an image.

  • 03

    Screen readers don’t announce images, and video titles don’t become headings. Readers have to be told about media in the question text.

Key writing decisions

The choices that shaped the article, and why I made them.

Test, don’t just cite

I tested each recommendation myself instead of repeating general guidance. The videos show the date picker failing with Voice Control and a screen reader entering an embedded form with and without a title, so readers can see the barrier, not just read about it.

Small choices, in their tool

Every tip is a change inside Google Forms, with the exact menu path, such as Settings > Presentation. Readers can follow along in their own form instead of translating abstract guidance.

Show the fix, not just the rule

Before-and-after examples for link text, 2 ways to point readers to a video, and 2 live embedded forms, one too short and one sized correctly, so the double-scrollbar problem is obvious.

Plain language over standards

I gave the contrast ratios and real color examples, like #767676 passing and #999999 failing, without leading with success criteria numbers. The audience needs to make a good form, not pass an audit.

Tell readers where to start

The takeaways open with the 2 changes that matter most: captions on important images and replacing the date picker. Then the rest of the checklist follows.

What I’d add next

A downloadable checklist version of the takeaways, so readers can keep it next to their form while they build.

A short note mapping each tip to its WCAG success criterion, for readers who need to document compliance as well as fix the form.
