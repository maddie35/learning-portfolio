Case study 03 · Security awareness

Cybersecurity awareness training

There was no cybersecurity policy and no training. I wrote the policy, then built the training that taught it — alongside the everyday habits it depends on: phishing awareness, password practice, handling organizational data.

My role

Policy author and learning designer — analysis, policy, build, evaluation

Audience

~15 board members, contractors and volunteers — limited-to-average prior knowledge

Format

Cybersecurity policy + live training + follow-up handout

Constraints

WCAG 2.1 AA, 30 minutes inside a board meeting, mixed personal devices

The problem

A small organization running on board members and volunteers, with real data to protect and nothing written down: no cybersecurity policy, no shared expectations, no training. Everyone was doing their careful best from memory.

So this was two jobs. First write a policy people could actually follow — short, plain, specific to how this group works. Then teach it in a way that changes behavior, not just a document everyone signs and forgets.

What analysis found

  • 01

    The real exposure was ordinary: shared logins, personal devices and email as the filing system. The policy had to speak to that, not to an enterprise IT setup nobody had.

  • 02

    There was no reporting path. People who noticed something odd sat on it, assuming someone else already knew — so "who you tell, and how fast" became a core objective.

  • 03

    Volunteers give their time in fragments, on whatever device they own. That set the constraints: under 30 minutes, resumable, keyboard-friendly, readable on a phone.

What I built

Foundation

The policy, in plain language

Two pages, not twenty: what we protect, what's expected of you, and what to do when something looks wrong. Written to be read once and remembered.

Core

Specifics, not definitions

Every point lands on something to do: the red flags that give a phish away, 15-character passphrases in a password manager, MFA turned on, auto-update enabled. WannaCry does the arguing about why patching matters.

Access

Accessible from the start

Keyboard-operable, full transcripts, text alternatives for every screenshot, no timed interactions, no color-only feedback — and a printable one-pager for people who'd rather pin it up.

Results

Kirkpatrick levels 1–4

LevelWhat was measuredWhat happened
L1 · ReactionPost-module feedbackBoard members and volunteers said it was the first security guidance that sounded like it was written for them. 4.9/5 satisfaction
L2 · LearningQuestions and discussion in sessionPasswords and file sharing drew the most questions, which shaped the action items in the follow-up email.
L3 · BehaviorReporting to leadershipVolunteers went from never reporting anything to proactively flagging suspicious messages to leadership. Observed rather than measured, because a volunteer organization this size has no incident tracking to point at.
L4 · ResultsPolicy in forceThe organization now has a cybersecurity policy everyone has been trained on, and a standing way to onboard the next volunteer.

What I'd do differently

I'd write the refresh cadence into the policy itself. I own the policy and the training, so it gets maintained — but that shouldn't depend on me remembering; it should be a date in the document.

I'd also add the example I cut for time: a suspicious request that appears to come from a board chair.
