Case study 01 · Program design

Engineering onboarding, built from scratch

One onboarding program for a global engineering organization at Affirm

Engineering onboarding didn’t exist as a program. A couple of teams had built their own, and everyone else improvised. I built one program for every engineering discipline across 10+ sites and 6 countries, and measured it on the number that mattered most to the business: time to shipping code.

My role

Program owner and learning designer: strategy, curriculum, facilitator enablement, evaluation

Audience

250+ new engineers in monthly cohorts of 18 to 35, from early-career engineers to senior managers

Format

Mobile-first Rise pre-learning, a pre-arrival session, and 3 days in person

Constraints

Volunteer SME facilitators with day jobs, and a product that changed constantly

The problem

A new SRE in one office and a new front-end engineer in another started with different information, different expectations, and different ideas about how the company worked.

Why it mattered: every week a new engineer spends ramping up is a week of salary before they ship. Inconsistent onboarding also meant every office taught a slightly different version of how Affirm works.

The goal: one set of guidelines, one version of the messaging, and a shared foundation across every discipline, from SREs and integration engineers to front-end and sales engineering.

What analysis found

  • 01

    There were no internal experts on onboarding, but there were experts on every subject onboarding needed to cover. The curriculum had to come from volunteer engineers, so delivery had to work without me in the room.

  • 02

    Everyone had opinions about onboarding, including the C-suite, and most arrived as proposals for new sessions. Three days is a fixed amount of room, so the program needed a clear way to decide what belonged in it.

  • 03

    Much of the technical content depended on current product behavior, and the product changed constantly. Without a system, content would go stale between monthly cohorts.

Key decisions and trade-offs

The choices that shaped the program, and what each one cost.

What goes where

I sorted every request into 3 places: the 3 days in person, for what needs the room and the cohort; pre-learning, for reference material; and "not an onboarding problem," for everything else. Saying no to a senior stakeholder is easier when the alternative is specific and the reasoning is about learner capacity, not preference.

Repeatable over heroic

I wrote facilitator guides and readiness checks, and ran train-the-trainer sessions before each cohort. The standard was that an engineer could deliver material they hadn’t designed, consistently. The trade-off: SME capacity became the limit on how fast the program could scale.

Maintenance as a queue

I built a pull-request monitor in Cursor that flagged product changes affecting the curriculum. Updates became a triggered queue instead of a periodic audit, so content went stale between cohorts far less often.

Let the pilot decide

I sat in on every pilot session and tracked where questions clustered. When the data showed a session doing 2 jobs at once, I split it instead of defending the original design.

What I built

Before day 1

Pre-learning and logistics

Articulate Rise modules published to the LMS, designed mobile-first for people completing them while traveling to the session. A short pre-arrival session covered getting into the building, what to bring, and what the days would look like.

Core

3 days in person

Sequenced from company fundamentals through discipline-specific technical content. Evening activities were built into the program, because the cohort relationships mattered as much as the content.

Scale

Facilitator enablement

Facilitator guides, readiness checks, and train-the-trainer sessions with 12+ volunteer engineering managers and senior engineers before every cohort.

What I’d do differently

I’d build a knowledge check into the design. A post-training survey tells you people valued it; it doesn’t tell you what they learned.

I’d run the pre-arrival logistics session from the first cohort. Several problems in that first group were avoidable and had nothing to do with instruction.

I’d certify a deeper bench of facilitators earlier. SME capacity was the constraint on scale, and a bigger bench would have made the monthly cadence less fragile.
