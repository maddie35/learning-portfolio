Essay 01

Universal Design as a Technical Writing Approach

Universal designTechnical writing

With over a decade in technical writing and editing, I've noticed significant overlap between Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles and technical documentation principles. Both tell you to use plain language. Both tell you not to assume what your reader already knows.

This overlap makes sense when you consider that the goal of both UDL and technical writing is to educate. UDL removes barriers in the learning process by ensuring equitable access and opportunity. Technical writers break complex information into language any user can act on, regardless of their technical background. Same goal, different deliverables.

When combined, UDL principles can help produce superior technical documentation.

Multiple means of representation

This is one that technical writing already half-does. Plain language, defined terms, consistent structure is standard practice in technical writing. What UDL adds is the expectation that a single representation is a barrier, not a default. The same procedure as prose, as a labeled diagram, and as a forty-second screen capture isn't redundancy; it's three different people succeeding where one previously did.

The prose reader is skimming for a specific step. The diagram reader needs to see how the parts relate before any step makes sense. The video reader is trying to confirm the interface actually looks like what you described. These are not the same person, and documentation that serves only the first one is making a choice it usually hasn't examined.

Multiple means of action and expression

This is the principle that technical writing most often ignores, and the one where I've seen the largest practical difference.

Most software tasks have more than one path. The GUI and the CLI, the bulk import and the one-at-a-time form, the admin console and the API. Documentation tends to pick one, usually the one the writer found first or the one the product team demoed. We do this for clarity and simplicity, but we also know that not every reader's situation will fit the happy path, forcing them to do translation work before they can start.

Finding a way to clearly document an alternate path is not a completeness exercise. It's the difference between a user who finishes and a user who files a ticket.

Multiple means of engagement

The hardest one to retrofit, because terseness gets enforced in review. Context reads as padding to anyone editing for length, so it's the first thing cut — and in traditional documentation environments, the priority is simply telling the user how, not why.

That holds up as long as looking it up works every time. It stops holding up when the system changes faster than anyone can re-learn it, which is now the normal case. Understanding transfers across a version change; a memorized sequence of clicks does not.

A reader arriving at a page is rarely asking only "how do I do this." They're asking: Should I do this? What happens if I get it wrong? Will I need to do this again? Stripping the why out of a procedure doesn't make it more efficient; it makes it something people follow without understanding, which is how you get correctly-executed steps applied to the wrong situation. One sentence of context before the steps is usually enough.

The short version

Universal design isn't a remediation pass you run over finished documentation. It's a constraint you accept at the start, which may cost real time and effort up front, but it also produces documentation that more people can actually use. Treating it as the baseline rather than the enhancement is mostly a matter of deciding to.

Further reading

  • The UDL Guidelines (CAST) — the primary source, organized by the three principles. Start here if you want the framework itself rather than someone's summary of it.
  • Federal Plain Language Guidelines — the most concrete, example-driven plain language reference I know of, and useful for winning arguments because it's a citable standard.
  • plainlanguage.gov — training material and before/after rewrites, good for showing a skeptical stakeholder what the change looks like in practice.
