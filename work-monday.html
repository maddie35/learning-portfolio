Case study 04 · Tool adoption

monday.com rollout training

A volunteer board adopting a work-management tool, with beginner-to-average computer confidence in the room and thirty minutes to work with. The goal wasn't coverage — it was "I can find my stuff and update it" before anyone left.

My role

Learning designer and facilitator — analysis, curriculum, live session, handout

Audience

Board members, contractors and volunteers — beginner-to-average, minimal tech vocabulary

Format

One 30-minute live session, hands-on, plus a one-page handout

Constraint

30 minutes, mixed confidence levels, in-room and remote at once

The problem

The obvious move was a feature tour: here's the sidebar, here are the column types, here's automations. For an audience with limited tech vocabulary and thirty minutes, that produces polite nodding and zero adoption.

Tool rollouts fail when people can't find their own work in week two. So the session had to buy confidence, not coverage — and confidence comes from doing it yourself while someone's standing there.

What analysis found

  • 01

    Beginner-to-average confidence and limited terminology meant vocabulary was a real barrier. "Board," "item," "group" and "column" had to be taught as words before anything could be taught as a task.

  • 02

    Only three things actually mattered in thirty minutes: find your stuff, update your stuff, read the dashboard. Everything else — building boards, automations — belonged in a later, optional session.

  • 03

    Confidence, not knowledge, was the thing to move — and the only way to know whether it moved was to ask. So the session ended with a knowledge check and a short survey rather than a show of hands.

What I built

Shape

Show, then they do

Every segment is a demo followed immediately by the same thing in their own hands, on a board they actually care about — not a sample project.

Core

Half the time on one thing

Fifteen of the thirty minutes go to updating and creating tasks, because everything else depends on it. Dashboards get seven; building boards gets a future session.

After

Checked, not assumed

A knowledge check during the session and a confidence survey after it, so "they seemed fine" wasn't the evidence. Both told me the hands-on time was doing the work.

Results

Kirkpatrick levels 1–4

LevelWhat was measuredWhat happened
L1 · ReactionPost-training survey4.8 out of 5 on "how confident do you feel creating your own board independently now?" — the hands-on stretch is what people named as the reason. 4.8/5 confidence
L2 · LearningKnowledge check + activity100% correct on the knowledge check, and every participant had added a real task, updated one assigned to them, and posted a comment before leaving. 100% correct
L3 · BehaviorOngoing useStatuses started moving between meetings rather than being reconstructed at them, and questions shifted from "where is it" to "how should we use it".
L4 · ResultsMeetings on shared statusBoard meetings could start from the dashboard instead of a round of verbal updates.

Levels 1 and 2 are measured; 3 and 4 are observed.

What I'd do differently

I'd run the confidence question again a month out. A 4.8 the moment you step away from a supported practice session is real, but it measures the session; what I want to know is whether it held once nobody was watching.

I'd also send the vocabulary ahead of time. The people with the least tech confidence would rather meet "board," "item" and "group" once in private than for the first time in a room.
