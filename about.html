About

Ten years explaining things. Eight making them learnable.

I came to learning design through technical writing, which is the best possible on-ramp. A decade of being handed something complicated and being responsible for whether a stranger could follow it. That's the same job as instructional design, with a different set of deliverables.

For the last eight years, I've built accessible content and learning experiences: eLearning modules, instructor-led curricula, job aids, assessments, and the process documentation that keeps all of it true after launch. Mostly for technical products, systems rollouts, and compliance topics people arrive at reluctantly.

My bias: the training is usually not the intervention. Analysis first, then the smallest thing that changes behavior, then a plan for what to remove next quarter.

What I believe about this work

Accessible design is just better design

Captions help the person in an open-plan office. Clear structure helps the person skimming at 4:55pm. Designing for the hardest case raises the floor for everyone — it isn't a remediation pass, it's the constraint that makes the work good.

Most training requests are diagnoses

Someone has already decided the problem is knowledge. Often it's a broken process, an unclear owner, or a tool nobody agreed on. Saying so early is more useful than building a beautiful module for the wrong problem.

Shorter is harder and worth it

Anyone can fill 45 minutes. Getting to the 12 minutes that change behavior takes analysis, argument with stakeholders, and a willingness to cut content you were proud of.

Measure the behavior, not the click

Completion rates measure compliance. I plan the evaluation before the build so there's something real to look at afterwards — and I'd rather report an honest qualitative result than a flattering number.
